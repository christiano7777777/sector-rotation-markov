\begin{abstract}
Traders describe sector rotation as money leaving one sector for another, but a matrix of pairwise conditional probabilities is not a valid model of it. We define the state on each day as the laggard, the sector with the lowest return, which gives a Markov chain on the eleven SPDR sector ETFs, estimated on daily data from 2018 to 2024. To separate structure from estimation noise, we derive the relevant benchmark from the circular law for random matrices with a variance profile. Without memory, the non-Perron eigenvalues of an estimated transition matrix fall within a disk of radius $\sqrt{(n-1)/N}$, 0.078 in our sample. A pre-specified family of three tests, whose power we measure on simulated chains, finds no first-order memory ($p = 0.36$, $0.21$ and $0.58$), although it would detect broad persistence or rotation of $|\lambda| \approx 0.05$. Out of sample, models that use past laggards forecast worse than the laggard's unconditional frequency. Dependence at lags of two to eight days is detectable in the sample but small, and it does not improve forecasts in the models we tested. The result holds across alternative estimators, state definitions and sampling schemes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

On a day when a large semiconductor company disappoints, the technology sector sells off and money appears to move into other sectors, financials or utilities, say. Traders call this sector rotation, and a natural way to model it is a matrix of conditional probabilities: given that sector $A$ fell today, how likely is sector $B$ to rise tomorrow? That matrix is not a Markov transition matrix. All eleven sectors move every day, so there is no single state for the market to be in, and the rows of such a matrix need not sum to one.

We fix the construction by collapsing each day to one state, the laggard, defined as the sector with the lowest return. This gives a valid Markov chain on eleven states and a precise version of the rotation question: does the identity of today's laggard tell us anything about tomorrow's? If it does, the chain has memory that a trader could use. If it does not, every row of the transition matrix equals the same distribution, and tomorrow's laggard is drawn afresh each day.

The answer matters either way. A laggard that predicts the next day's laggard would be a simple signal on some of the most liquid funds in the market. A laggard that does not would mean that a model of daily rotation needs more information than which sector fell today.

Answering the question is mainly a problem of benchmarks. An estimated transition matrix always shows some structure, because its rows are estimated from finite samples, and the question is whether that structure is larger than estimation noise. The benchmark most often used for random matrices in finance, the Marchenko--Pastur law \cite{marchenkopastur1967}, applies to covariance matrices and not to transition matrices. A circular law for random Markov matrices \cite{bordenave2012} does apply to transition matrices, but it describes a matrix whose rows are uniformly random rather than estimated from data, and its radius is too wide for a sample of our size.

This paper makes three contributions. First, it sets out a legitimate state space for sector rotation and estimates the resulting chain on the eleven SPDR sector ETFs from 2018 to 2024. Second, it derives the noise benchmark for an estimated transition matrix from the circular law for random matrices with a variance profile \cite{cooketal2018}. Without memory, the non-Perron eigenvalues fall within a disk of radius $\sqrt{(n-1)/N}$, where $n$ is the number of states and $N$ the number of transitions. Third, it tests the chain with a pre-specified family of three tests whose power is measured by simulation, and checks the answer with out-of-sample forecasts.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This paper draws on three literatures: Markov and regime-switching models of markets, empirical work on sector rotation, and random matrix theory in finance. It also uses standard tools from forecast evaluation.

\subsection{Markov and regime-switching models}

Modelling a financial series as moving between a small number of discrete states became common after Hamilton \cite{hamilton1989}, whose Markov-switching autoregression is now the standard tool for dating business-cycle regimes. In that literature the state, such as expansion or recession, is latent and inferred from a continuous series by maximum likelihood. Our state, the daily laggard, is observed directly, so the transition matrix can be estimated by counting transitions rather than by filtering. The two approaches therefore face different risks. Regime models must deal with misclassified states, while the main risk for our chain is defining a legitimate state in the first place, which Section~\ref{sec:state} addresses.

\subsection{Sector rotation}

Sector rotation is better established as a practitioner heuristic than as an empirical regularity. Moskowitz and Grinblatt \cite{moskowitzgrinblatt1999} show that industry momentum accounts for much of the momentum in individual stocks, so sectors do show persistent returns at monthly to annual horizons. Tested as a market-timing strategy, the evidence is weaker. Stangl et al.\ \cite{stangletal2009} find that rotating across sectors with perfect foresight of the business-cycle stage, and without transaction costs, would have earned at most 2.3\% a year over the market from 1948 to 2007, and Molchanov and Stangl \cite{molchanovstangl2024} find no systematic evidence of the sector performance patterns that conventional rotation advice predicts across the cycle. These papers ask whether rotation timed to the business cycle is profitable. We ask a narrower question, whether the day-to-day identity of the laggard carries any memory, independent of any macro signal. Given the monthly momentum documented by Moskowitz and Grinblatt \cite{moskowitzgrinblatt1999}, a null result at the daily horizon need not conflict with persistence at longer horizons.

\subsection{Random matrix theory in finance}

The idea that an estimated financial matrix should be compared with its random-matrix null before it is interpreted dates to Laloux et al.\ \cite{lalouxetal1999} and Plerou et al.\ \cite{plerouetal2000}, who showed that most eigenvalues of empirical stock-return correlation matrices are indistinguishable from the Marchenko--Pastur spectrum of pure noise \cite{marchenkopastur1967}; Bouchaud and Potters \cite{bouchaudpotters2009} review later work. That literature studies symmetric correlation matrices, and Section~\ref{sec:mp} explains why the same benchmark does not apply to a transition matrix. For random Markov matrices, Bordenave et al.\ \cite{bordenave2012} prove a circular law: when the rows are drawn independently and uniformly, the non-Perron eigenvalues fill a disk of radius $1/\sqrt n$, which we call the Dirichlet radius. An estimated transition matrix is a different object, a fixed matrix plus estimation noise whose variance differs across entries. Cook et al.\ \cite{cooketal2018} give the limiting spectrum of random matrices with such a variance profile, and Section~\ref{sec:radius} applies their result to obtain the noise radius for our chain. As far as we are aware, sector rotation has not previously been tested against a random-matrix benchmark.

\subsection{Forecast evaluation}

A statistically significant pattern in the sample need not improve forecasts. We therefore evaluate out-of-sample forecasts with the log score, a strictly proper scoring rule that rewards a forecast for the probability it gives to the outcome that occurs \cite{gneitingraftery2007}, and compare forecasters with the test of equal predictive accuracy of Diebold and Mariano \cite{dieboldmariano1995}, using the variance estimator of Newey and West \cite{neweywest1987} to allow for serial correlation in the score differences.

\section{Constructing a Legitimate State Space}
\label{sec:state}

\subsection{Why a pairwise conditional-probability matrix fails}

Consider a matrix $M$ with entries $M_{AB} = \mathbb P(r_{B,t+1} > 0 \mid r_{A,t} < 0)$, the probability that sector $B$ rises tomorrow given that sector $A$ fell today, estimated over all pairs of sectors. $M$ cannot serve as a Markov transition matrix, for two reasons.

The first is that there is no single current state. A Markov chain needs a state $S_t$ that the system occupies at each time $t$, with transitions $S_t \to S_{t+1}$. On any trading day, however, all $n$ sectors move at once, so the position of the market on day $t$ is the return vector $(r_{1,t},\dots,r_{n,t})$ rather than one categorical label. $M$ treats every sector as the current state at the same time. A fall in sector $A$ does not stop the other sectors from rising or falling on the same day, so there is no unique antecedent to condition on.

The second is that the rows of $M$ need not sum to one. For a fixed sector $A$, the events ``$B$ rises the next day'' for different $B \neq A$ are not mutually exclusive, because several sectors can rise together, or none can. Summing $M_{AB}$ over $B$ therefore counts joint outcomes more than once, and a row can sum to more or less than one depending on how correlated the sectors were in the sample. Rescaling the rows would make $M$ row-stochastic, but the result would have no meaning as a transition law.

\subsection{A single categorical state}

We collapse each period to one state by defining
\begin{equation}
S_t \;=\; \arg\min_{i \in \{1,\dots,n\}} r_{i,t},
\label{eq:laggard}
\end{equation}
the laggard sector, that is, the sector with the lowest return in period $t$. Because the $\arg\min$ selects one index, the market occupies exactly one of $n$ states in each period, and
\begin{equation}
P_{ij} \;=\; \mathbb P(S_{t+1} = j \mid S_t = i)
\end{equation}
is a genuine transition probability.

Unlike $M$, $P$ is a row-stochastic matrix by construction, with non-negative entries and rows that sum to one (Definition~\ref{def:stoch}, Appendix~\ref{app:defs}). Exactly one transition $S_t \to S_{t+1}$ is observed in each period, so the transition counts $n_{ij}$ used to estimate $P$ (Section~\ref{sec:estimation}) partition the sample, and each row sums to one. A table such as $M$ still answers a valid question about a pair of sectors, how often $B$ rises the day after $A$ falls. It does not describe how the market as a whole moves from one state to the next.

Three details of the construction matter for what follows. First, we use total returns, which include dividends. Price returns fall on each fund's ex-dividend date by roughly the dividend paid, so high-yield sectors such as Utilities and Real Estate would be recorded as laggards on those dates for a reason unrelated to trading. Second, ties are possible because returns are reported to six decimal places. Our sample contains one, on 2019-06-20, when Health Care and Financials both returned 0.4396\%. We assign the tie to the sector listed first, and breaking it the other way leaves every conclusion unchanged (Section~\ref{sec:robust}). Third, replacing the $\arg\min$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:laggard} with an $\arg\max$ defines a leader chain, which we use as a check on the choice of state.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

We use the eleven SPDR Select Sector ETFs as tradable proxies for the GICS sectors: Technology (XLK), Health Care (XLV), Financials (XLF), Consumer Discretionary (XLY), Communication Services (XLC), Industrials (XLI), Consumer Staples (XLP), Energy (XLE), Utilities (XLU), Real Estate (XLRE) and Materials (XLB). Daily total returns, which are split-adjusted and include dividends, come from the CRSP daily stock file \cite{crsp} for 2010-01-01 to 2024-12-31. Funds are matched to their CRSP identifiers (PERMNOs) through the CRSP name history, giving 37{,}934 sector-day observations.

The data pass basic checks. Two return observations are missing (0.005\% of the sample), no daily return exceeds 50\% in absolute value, and there are no duplicate sector-date pairs. As a check on the dividend adjustment, CRSP's total return has a correlation of 0.995 with a price-only return rebuilt from the price series, and the remaining gap is consistent with dividends, which only the total return includes.

The panel is unbalanced. XLC began trading in June 2018 and XLRE in October 2015, while the other nine funds trade throughout. The laggard is only comparable across days if it is chosen from the same set of sectors, so we restrict the chain to the period in which all eleven funds trade, 2018-06-20 to 2024-12-31. This common period has 1{,}644 trading days and 1{,}643 transitions. For the robustness checks in Section~\ref{sec:robust} we also form weekly returns, compounded from Friday to Friday over the same period (342 weeks, 341 transitions).

All numbers, tables and figures in this paper are produced by a single notebook from the extracted returns. The notebook fixes the random seed, so a rerun reproduces every result exactly, and it checks the transition counts against Table~\ref{tab:counts} before any test is run.

\section{Estimation}
\label{sec:estimation}

\subsection{Maximum likelihood and Laplace smoothing}

We count the number of days on which the laggard moves from each sector to each other sector. The maximum-likelihood estimate $\hat P$ of each transition probability is the share of transitions out of a sector that go to a given sector. Our main specification instead uses the Laplace-smoothed estimate $\tilde P$, which adds one pseudo-count ($\alpha = 1$) to every cell before taking shares. Appendix~\ref{app:defs} gives the likelihood and both estimators. The full count matrix and $\tilde P$ are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:matrices}, and a three-state example of both estimators is worked through in Appendix~\ref{app:toy}.

\subsection{Free parameters and data adequacy}
\label{sec:sparsity}

An $n$-state chain has $n^2$ transition probabilities, but each row loses one degree of freedom because it must sum to one, leaving $n(n-1)$ free parameters. With $n=11$ this is 110. A common rule of thumb asks for 10 to 20 transitions per parameter, that is, 1{,}100 to 2{,}200 transitions. The daily sample has 1{,}643 (14.9 per parameter), which is adequate, while the weekly sample has only 341 (3.1 per parameter). We therefore use daily data for the main analysis and keep the weekly chain as a robustness check.

The data are also spread unevenly across rows. Energy is the laggard on 387 of the 1{,}643 transitions, Industrials on only 45, and only one of the 121 cells is empty: Industrials never follows itself. Because the other ten diagonal entries are positive and every other transition is observed, the raw estimate $\hat P$ is already irreducible and aperiodic, so smoothing is not needed to make the chain ergodic.

\subsection{Why the smoothing choice does not affect inference}

Smoothing does change the point estimates. One pseudo-count per cell adds 11 observations to each row, about 3\% of the Energy row but about 20\% of the Industrials row, and it shrinks every row towards the uniform distribution. The stationary probability of Energy falls from 0.2355 under $\hat P$ to 0.2256 under $\tilde P$, and that of Industrials rises from 0.0274 to 0.0317. We therefore report the raw frequencies alongside the smoothed estimates in Section~\ref{sec:chain}.

Smoothing does not drive the test results. Every null distribution in Sections~\ref{sec:null} and \ref{sec:tests} is built by applying the same estimator to sequences without memory, so the shrinkage applies equally to the data and to the null. Repeating the main test with $\alpha = 0$, $\alpha = 0.5$, and with pseudo-counts placed in proportion to the stationary distribution instead of uniformly moves the largest non-Perron modulus between 0.084 and 0.092, while its permutation $p$-value stays between 0.21 and 0.22 (Section~\ref{sec:robust}).

\section{The Estimated Chain}
\label{sec:chain}

The estimated chain is ergodic, and it is close to the rank-one matrix $\mathbf 1\pi$ in which every row equals the stationary distribution. In such a chain tomorrow's laggard does not depend on today's, which is the hypothesis Sections~\ref{sec:null} and \ref{sec:tests} test formally.

\subsection{Ergodicity}

By the Perron--Frobenius theorem, a chain that is irreducible and aperiodic has a unique stationary distribution $\pi$ and converges to it from any starting state (Proposition~\ref{prop:pf}, Appendix~\ref{app:defs}). Both conditions hold for $\tilde P$. Every entry of $\tilde P$ is positive, with a minimum of 0.0179, so the chain is irreducible in one step and aperiodic because every state can return to itself in one step. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:sparsity}, the same holds for $\hat P$, whose only zero is the Industrials diagonal.

\subsection{Stationary distribution and recurrence}

We compute $\pi$ as the left eigenvector of $\tilde P$ for eigenvalue 1. Table~\ref{tab:pi} reports it next to the raw laggard frequencies, together with the mean recurrence time, which is the reciprocal of the stationary probability \cite{kemenysnell1976}. Energy is by far the most frequent laggard ($\pi = 0.2256$, raw frequency 0.2354), returning on average every 4.4 trading days. Industrials is the rarest ($\pi = 0.0317$, raw frequency 0.0274), returning every 31.5 days, or every 36.5 days on the raw frequency. Smoothing matters most for rare states, which is why both columns are shown. A parametric bootstrap from $\hat P$ (1{,}000 simulated sequences of the same length) gives 95\% intervals of $[0.207, 0.246]$ for the stationary probability of Energy and $[0.025, 0.039]$ for Industrials.

\subsection{Hitting times}

The expected hitting time $H_{ij}$ is the mean number of days until the laggard first reaches sector $j$ starting from sector $i$; it solves a linear system given in Appendix~\ref{app:diag}. Averaged over starting sectors $i \neq j$, the laggard reaches Energy in 4.55 trading days (95\% interval $[4.16, 5.00]$) and Industrials in 31.15 days ($[25.4, 40.0]$). The full matrix (Table~\ref{tab:hitting}) has almost constant columns: the time to reach Energy ranges only from 4.3 to 4.7 days across the ten starting sectors. If every row of $P$ equalled $\pi$, the time to reach $j$ would be geometric with mean $1/\pi_j$ whatever the starting sector, and the columns would be exactly constant. The hitting times are close to that benchmark, which is a first sign that the starting state carries little information.

\subsection{Spectrum and mixing}

Table~\ref{tab:eig} lists the eigenvalues of $\tilde P$. Apart from the Perron eigenvalue $\lambda_0 = 1$, the largest in modulus is real, $\lambda_1 = 0.0844$, so the spectral gap, one minus $|\lambda_1|$, is 0.916 (95\% interval $[0.854, 0.919]$) and the relaxation time, its reciprocal, is 1.09 trading days. Three complex pairs imply cycles of 2.3, 3.6 and 14.2 trading days, but their moduli are at most 0.069, so any such cycle loses over 99\% of its amplitude within two days.

\section{The Correct Null}
\label{sec:null}

The small eigenvalues in Table~\ref{tab:eig} could be weak structure or estimation noise. Deciding between the two needs a benchmark for how large the eigenvalues of an estimated transition matrix are when there is no structure at all.

\subsection{Why the Marchenko--Pastur law does not apply}
\label{sec:mp}

Random matrix theory entered empirical finance through the Marchenko--Pastur law \cite{marchenkopastur1967}, which gives the eigenvalue distribution of a sample covariance matrix built from pure noise and has been used to separate signal from noise in stock-return correlation matrices \cite{lalouxetal1999,plerouetal2000}. That law describes a symmetric, positive semi-definite matrix with real, non-negative eigenvalues. A transition matrix is none of these. It is not symmetric, its eigenvalues are in general complex, its rows are constrained to sum to one, and one eigenvalue is fixed at 1. The Marchenko--Pastur law therefore has no bearing on $\tilde P$; Appendix~\ref{app:mp} sets out the mismatch in full.

\subsection{The noise radius}
\label{sec:radius}

Under the null of no memory, every row of $P$ equals $\pi$, and all non-Perron eigenvalues are exactly zero. What we observe is an estimate equal to that matrix plus estimation noise, and the non-Perron eigenvalues of the estimate are exactly the eigenvalues of the noise. The noise in each entry has mean zero and a variance that depends on how many transitions its row contains and on how frequent its column sector is. Cook et al.\ \cite{cooketal2018} show that the eigenvalues of a random matrix with such a variance profile fill a disk whose radius is set by the profile. For an estimated transition matrix with $n$ states and $N$ transitions, the radius is
\begin{equation}
r = \sqrt{\frac{n-1}{N}}.
\label{eq:radius}
\end{equation}
Appendix~\ref{app:radius} derives this result. With $n = 11$ and $N = 1{,}643$, $r = 0.078$. The radius does not depend on $\pi$, so the uneven row sizes in Section~\ref{sec:sparsity} need no adjustment. It is close to one over the square root of the average number of transitions per row, 0.082, and it matches the mean of the largest non-Perron modulus under our permutation null, 0.076.

The benchmark usually cited for random Markov matrices is the circular law with radius $1/\sqrt n$ \cite{bordenave2012}. That result describes a matrix whose rows are drawn from a uniform Dirichlet distribution, which behaves like a row estimated from about $n$ observations. It is the special case of Eq.~\eqref{eq:radius} in which each row has about $n$ transitions. Our rows have about 149 transitions each, so the Dirichlet radius of 0.30 is almost four times too wide, and a test against it would miss any structure smaller than that. A Monte Carlo check confirms the Dirichlet scale (mean largest modulus 0.182, 95th percentile 0.307).

Two caveats limit how Eq.~\eqref{eq:radius} can be used. The noise entries within a row are not independent, because each row of $\hat P$ sums to one, and the theorem is asymptotic. We therefore use $r$ as the scale of the noise and base inference on the permutation null below.

Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum} places the eigenvalues of $\tilde P$ against these benchmarks. The largest, 0.084, lies just outside the asymptotic radius but inside the 95\% band of the permutation null.

\subsection{Permutation null}
\label{sec:perm}

For exact finite-sample inference we shuffle the observed laggard sequence 5{,}000 times. Each shuffle keeps the marginal frequency of every sector but destroys any dependence on time. We apply the same estimator, including the smoothing, to each shuffled sequence, and the $p$-value of a statistic is the share of shuffles in which it is at least as large as in the data. With 5{,}000 shuffles the Monte Carlo standard error of a $p$-value near 0.21 is about 0.006.

\section{Tests and Power}
\label{sec:tests}

\subsection{Pre-specified test family}

We test the null of no first-order memory with three statistics, specified before looking at the results. The primary test is the $\chi^2$ test of independence between $S_t$ and $S_{t+1}$ on the $11 \times 11$ table of transition counts \cite{andersongoodman1957}, which is the direct test of a memoryless (order-0) chain against an order-1 chain. Only 15\% of its expected cell counts fall below 5, so its asymptotic distribution is a reasonable guide. The two secondary tests use the spectrum of $\tilde P$. The largest non-Perron modulus $|\lambda_1|$ responds to a single dominant mode of persistence or rotation, and the trace statistic, the sum of the squared non-Perron moduli, accumulates many small modes. Appendix~\ref{app:defs} defines all three statistics and the permutation $p$-value. Both are judged against the permutation null.

\subsection{Power}
\label{sec:power}

A null result only carries information if the tests could have found a signal. We measure power by planting known structure in simulated chains with the same length and stationary distribution as the data. Each planted chain mixes the memoryless chain with a structured matrix $W$, putting weight $a$ on $W$ (Appendix~\ref{app:defs}), where $W$ is the identity (every sector tends to stay the laggard), an 11-cycle permutation (the laggard rotates through the sectors in a fixed order), or a matrix that adds persistence to a single sector, Energy or Industrials. For each signal size we simulate 300 sequences of 1{,}643 transitions and apply the three tests with critical values from 2{,}000 sequences without memory.

\subsection{Results}

None of the three tests rejects the null. The $\chi^2$ statistic is 104.6 on 100 degrees of freedom ($p = 0.357$; the likelihood-ratio version gives $G^2 = 107.9$, $p = 0.277$). The largest non-Perron modulus, 0.0844, has a permutation $p$-value of 0.210 against a 95\% critical value of 0.097, and the trace statistic has $p = 0.581$. The number of complex pairs, three, is also typical of the null (mean 3.5, $p = 0.952$). Figure~\ref{fig:perm} compares the six largest moduli with their permutation distributions; per-rank $p$-values for all ten are in Appendix~\ref{app:matrices}.

They cannot rule out weaker signals, or persistence confined to one sector below $|\lambda| \approx 0.11$.

\clearpage
\section{Robustness}
\label{sec:robust}

The absence of first-order memory holds under every specification we tried. The only dependence we find is at lags of two days or more, and it is small.

\subsection{Markov order}
\label{sec:order}

We compare chains of order 0 (memoryless), 1 and 2 on the same sample, starting from the third day so that all three models predict the same transitions. Both information criteria select order 0: the Akaike criterion is 7{,}412.5, 7{,}505.0 and 8{,}508.7 for orders 0, 1 and 2, and the Bayesian criterion is 7{,}466.6, 8{,}099.4 and 14{,}993.1.

The likelihood-ratio test of order 2 against order 1 needs more care. Its statistic is 1{,}176.2 on 1{,}090 degrees of freedom, which gives a nominal $p$-value of 0.035, but most of the 1{,}331 cells of the order-2 table are sparse, so the $\chi^2$ approximation is unreliable. We therefore calibrate the statistic by parametric bootstrap, simulating 2{,}000 order-1 chains of the same length. The answer depends on which matrix the chains are simulated from. From $\hat P$ the null mean is 1{,}060 and $p = 0.004$; from $\tilde P$ the null mean is 1{,}110 and $p = 0.058$. The difference arises because $\hat P$ contains a structural zero and sharper rows, which lower the null distribution. A permutation that shuffles the sequence only within 21-day blocks gives $p = 0.134$. Taken together, these results are borderline evidence of second-order dependence. Section~\ref{sec:lags} traces it to dependence at a lag of two days, and the order test on three macro states in Section~\ref{sec:spec}, where the $\chi^2$ approximation is valid, gives $p = 0.045$ for the main grouping and 0.263 for the alternative.

\subsection{Stability over time}
\label{sec:stability}

We test whether the transition law differs between turbulent and calm periods with a likelihood ratio that compares separate maximum-likelihood estimates for the two regimes with a pooled estimate. The $p$-value comes from 2{,}000 circular shifts of the regime indicator, which keep the length and clustering of each regime but break its link to the laggard sequence. For the crisis windows of February to April 2020, January to June 2022 and March 2023 (209 transitions), $p = 0.75$. For a split chosen by the data, the top third of days by the lagged 21-day realised volatility of an equal-weighted sector portfolio (541 transitions), $p = 0.87$. We also repeat the largest-modulus test in 67 rolling windows of 252 days, advanced by 21 days. None of the windows rejects at the 5\% level, and the smallest $p$-value is 0.097, for the window ending on 2023-02-21.

These tests have limited power. A 252-day window has about 23 transitions per row, so its noise radius from Eq.~\eqref{eq:radius} is close to 0.20, and the 95\% critical values of the windows range from 0.17 to 0.20. They can rule out a large local signal but not a weak one. The results show no instability large enough to detect, which is not the same as showing that the transition law is constant.

The state checks address the choice of label. The volatility-standardised laggard divides each return by its own volatility, estimated with an exponentially weighted average of past squared returns (decay 0.94) and known on the previous day, after a 60-day burn-in. This makes the label less a ranking of which sector is most volatile: on the 1{,}584 days left after the 60-day burn-in, Energy's share falls from 0.237 to 0.181, and the ratio of the most to the least frequent laggard falls from 8.5 to 4.4. The macro states collapse the eleven sectors into three groups fixed in advance: Defensive (Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities, Real Estate), Growth (Technology, Communication, Consumer Discretionary) and Cyclical (Energy, Materials, Industrials, Financials). The alternative grouping moves Real Estate and Consumer Discretionary into Cyclical. With three states the order-2 model has 18 parameters, no expected cell count falls below 5, and the order-2 test gives $\chi^2 = 21.4$ on 12 degrees of freedom ($p = 0.045$) for the main grouping and $p = 0.263$ for the alternative.

The sampling checks address the calendar. A Friday-to-Monday transition spans three calendar days and a post-holiday transition spans more, so the consecutive-days check keeps only transitions between adjacent calendar days. The option-expiry check drops every transition that starts or ends on the third Friday of a month. The laggard is also unrelated to the day of the week ($\chi^2 = 41.3$ on 40 degrees of freedom, $p = 0.41$), a static check that we list separately because it does not test memory.

\subsection{Dependence at longer lags}
\label{sec:lags}

A first-order test cannot see dependence that skips a day, so we also test $S_{t-k}$ against $S_t$ for $k = 1,\dots,20$ with the $\chi^2$ statistic and 1{,}000 permutations each. Lag 1 is again unremarkable ($p = 0.354$). Lags 2, 4 and 8 have $p$-values of 0.016, 0.001 and 0.022. Three of twenty below 0.05 is more than the one expected by chance, but after controlling the false discovery rate at 5\% \cite{benjaminihochberg1995} only lag 4 remains. Lag 4 is significant in both halves of the sample ($p = 0.027$ and 0.044), and a permutation within 21-day blocks, which holds fixed any slow drift in the laggard frequencies, gives $p = 0.006$ for lag 4, 0.054 for lag 2 and 0.048 for lag 8. The lag-2 dependence also appears in both macro groupings ($p = 0.018$ and 0.025), while lag 4 appears only in the main grouping ($p = 0.027$ against 0.341).

The dependence is small. Cram\'er's $V$ for lag 4 is 0.095, against 0.078 expected under no dependence, and the excess is 0.012 at lag 2 and 0.002 at lag 1. It also weakens for the volatility-standardised state (lag 4, $p = 0.064$) and is not significant for the leader chain ($p = 0.144$), which suggests that part of it reflects volatility that persists for several days. Section~\ref{sec:oos} asks whether it is large enough to forecast with.

\section{Out-of-Sample Predictability}
\label{sec:oos}

A Markov-1 model and a lag-4 model both forecast tomorrow's laggard significantly worse than a forecast that uses only the laggard's unconditional frequency.

\subsection{Design}

From day 504 onward, about two years into the sample, we refit each model on the data up to day $t$ and forecast the distribution of $S_{t+1}$. The benchmark is the frequency forecast, the smoothed frequency of each sector so far, which is the best forecast if the chain has no memory. The competitors are a Markov-1 model (the $\alpha = 1$ row of $S_t$), the same model with its rows shrunk towards the frequency forecast by 11 pseudo-counts, and a lag-4 model (the $\alpha = 1$ row of $S_{t-3}$ in the lag-4 count table). We score each forecast by the log of the probability given to the sector that did lag, and compare models with the Diebold--Mariano test \cite{dieboldmariano1995} on the daily difference in log scores, using a Newey--West variance with five lags \cite{neweywest1987}. We report three designs: an expanding window from day 504 (1{,}139 forecasts, 2020-06-23 to 2024-12-31), a rolling window of 504 days, and an expanding window from day 252 (1{,}391 forecasts).

\subsection{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:oos} shows that every model with memory loses to the frequency forecast, in every design, with $t$-statistics between $-2.35$ and $-6.61$. For reference, a uniform guess scores $\log(1/11) = -2.398$, so the frequency forecast gains about 0.14 nats a day by knowing that Energy lags most often, and the memory models give part of that back. The hit rates are close, and the Brier score \cite{brier1950} favours the frequency forecast. In the first design the frequency forecast picks the right sector on 23.4\% of days, the same as always predicting Energy, against 23.5\% for Markov-1 and 22.4\% for lag-4, and its Brier score is lower (0.880 against 0.886 and 0.884).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why a null result is plausible}

A null result fits how the funds are built and traded. In a capitalisation-weighted fund, weights move with prices, so a fall in one sector does not force the fund to buy or sell anything the next day. Products whose rules do force trading after a price move, such as daily-rebalanced leveraged funds, are not part of our sample, and we do not test whether their flows reach sector returns. The Select Sector indices do trade mechanically at times. Their capping rules limit the weight of the largest companies and are applied at quarterly rebalances, and at its June 2024 rebalance the technology fund sold roughly \$10 billion of Apple and bought nearly as much Nvidia, according to a Morningstar analyst quoted by Shaw \cite{shaw2024}. S\&P changed the capping method in September 2024 to avoid such swings. These trades fall on a few known dates, however, and move money between companies within one sector, so they would not create day-to-day dependence in which sector lags. A second reason is that the sector funds are liquid and widely traded. A laggard pattern strong enough to forecast with would be easy to trade, and would plausibly not survive for long.

\subsection{The laggard partly ranks volatility}

The laggard is the sector with the lowest return, and a more volatile sector is more likely to have the lowest return, and the highest, on any given day. Energy is the most volatile sector in our sample, with annualised volatility of 33\% against 26\% for Technology, the next highest. It is also both the most frequent laggard ($\pi = 0.226$) and the most frequent leader (stationary probability 0.209 in the leader chain). Standardising returns by their own volatility lowers Energy's share of laggard days from 0.237 to 0.181 on the 1{,}584 days left after the 60-day burn-in and halves the spread between the most and least frequent laggard (Section~\ref{sec:spec}). Part of what the chain measures is therefore which sector moves most, not which sector is out of favour. The memory tests stay null under both labels, so this affects the interpretation of $\pi$ rather than the main result, but it also suggests that some of the lag dependence in Section~\ref{sec:lags} reflects volatility that persists for several days.

\subsection{Limitations}

The state is coarse. It records only which sector had the lowest return, not by how much or in what order the others finished, so a model of return magnitudes or full rankings could find structure that this chain cannot.

The tests have limits on what they can detect. Broad persistence or rotation of $|\lambda| \approx 0.05$ would be detected with 80\% power, but persistence confined to one sector needs $|\lambda| \approx 0.11$ to $0.15$ (Table~\ref{tab:power}), and the stability tests can only detect local signals of about 0.2 (Section~\ref{sec:stability}). A weak signal that is confined to one sector or to a short period could therefore have been missed.

We document the dependence at lags of two to eight days but do not model it. A full order-2 chain on eleven states has 1{,}210 free parameters (1{,}200 for the 120 pairs that occur), more than our sample can estimate, and the three-state version in Section~\ref{sec:spec} gives only borderline evidence. Section~\ref{sec:oos} suggests that modelling it would not help forecasts, but a more parsimonious model could do better than the lag-4 table we tested.

The sectors themselves change over the sample. The September 2018 GICS revision created Communication Services and moved Alphabet and Facebook out of Information Technology \cite{msci2018}, a change the S\&P sector indices adopted on 24 September 2018 \cite{thinkadvisor2018}, and the revision of 17 March 2023 moved Visa and Mastercard from Information Technology to Financials \cite{spdji2023}. Each changes what a sector label refers to, and the stability tests have limited power to detect a resulting shift in the transition law. Large one-off flows, such as the 2024 rebalance of the technology fund, raise a similar concern for shorter stretches of the sample.

Finally, we study the daily horizon, with only a weekly check (Section~\ref{sec:spec}). Industry momentum has been documented at monthly to annual horizons \cite{moskowitzgrinblatt1999}, and our result does not speak to those.

\clearpage
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether today's worst-performing S\&P 500 sector tells us which sector will be worst tomorrow. Defining the laggard as a single state per day gives a valid Markov chain, and from 2018 to 2024 that chain shows no first-order memory. A $\chi^2$ test of independence and two spectral tests against the correct noise benchmark all fail to reject, and simulations show that they would have detected broad persistence or rotation of $|\lambda| \approx 0.05$. Out of sample, models that use the previous laggard forecast worse than the laggard's unconditional frequency. There is detectable dependence at lags of two to eight days, but it is small and does not improve forecasts in the models we tested.

The methodological point is that a null result is only informative when the benchmark and the power are right. The Marchenko--Pastur law does not apply to a transition matrix, and the Dirichlet radius is almost four times too wide for an estimated matrix of this size. Against the radius $\sqrt{(n-1)/N}$ and a test family with measured power, the absence of structure in the daily laggard is a finding rather than a failure to look.

\section{Worked Three-State Example}
\label{app:toy}

This example uses three states, Technology (T), Financials (F) and Energy (E), to show the estimators of Section~\ref{sec:estimation} and the quantities of Section~\ref{sec:chain} on numbers small enough to check by hand. It illustrates the calculations only and plays no part in the tests.

\paragraph{Estimation.} Suppose the laggard over 13 periods is T T T E T F T F E F F T F. The 12 transitions give the counts below, in the order $(T, F, E)$:
\begin{equation*}
(n_{ij}) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\hat P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.333 & 0.500 & 0.167 \\ 0.500 & 0.250 & 0.250 \\ 0.500 & 0.500 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
Energy is never followed by Energy, so $\hat P_{EE} = 0$. With two transitions out of Energy this zero is a small-sample artefact rather than evidence that the transition is impossible. Laplace smoothing with $\alpha = 1$ replaces the Energy row by $(1+1,\ 1+1,\ 0+1)/(2+3) = (0.4,\ 0.4,\ 0.2)$.

\paragraph{Stationary distribution.} For the illustrative matrix
\begin{equation*}
P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.50 & 0.50 & 0 \\ 0.25 & 0.50 & 0.25 \\ 0 & 0.50 & 0.50 \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation*}
solving $\pi P = \pi$ with $\sum_i \pi_i = 1$ gives $\pi = (0.25,\ 0.50,\ 0.25)$, so the mean recurrence times are $(4,\ 2,\ 4)$ periods: Financials is the laggard twice as often as Technology or Energy.

\paragraph{Complex eigenvalues and rotation.} The chain $P = 0.3\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top/3 + 0.7\,\Pi_3$, where $\Pi_3$ is the 3-cycle $T \to F \to E \to T$, has rows $(0.1, 0.8, 0.1)$, $(0.1, 0.1, 0.8)$ and $(0.8, 0.1, 0.1)$. Its non-Perron eigenvalues are $-0.35 \pm 0.606i$, with modulus 0.70 and argument $\pm 120^\circ$. The argument gives a cycle of $360/120 = 3$ periods, matching the 3-cycle, and the modulus gives the damping: the spectral gap is 0.30 and the relaxation time $1/0.30 = 3.3$ periods. This is the kind of structure that a rotation would leave in the spectrum. A modulus of 0.70 is far above anything in Table~\ref{tab:eig}, but a three-state toy chain says nothing about the power of our tests on the real sample; Section~\ref{sec:power} measures that directly.

\section{Definitions, Estimators and Test Statistics}
\label{app:defs}

\begin{definition}[Row-stochastic matrix]
\label{def:stoch}
An $n \times n$ matrix $P$ is a (row-)stochastic transition matrix if and only if
\begin{equation}
\text{(i)}\quad P_{ij} \ge 0 \ \ \forall i,j, \qquad\qquad \text{(ii)}\quad \sum_{j=1}^n P_{ij} = 1 \ \ \forall i.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Estimators.} Let $n_{ij}$ be the number of days on which the laggard moves from sector $i$ to sector $j$, $n_{i\cdot} = \sum_j n_{ij}$ the number of transitions out of $i$, and $N = \sum_i n_{i\cdot}$. Conditional on the first state, the likelihood of the observed sequence is $\prod_{i,j} P_{ij}^{n_{ij}}$, which is maximised row by row at
\begin{equation}
\hat P_{ij} = \frac{n_{ij}}{n_{i\cdot}}.
\end{equation}
The Laplace-smoothed estimator adds a pseudo-count $\alpha$ to every cell,
\begin{equation}
\tilde P_{ij} = \frac{n_{ij} + \alpha}{n_{i\cdot} + \alpha n}, \qquad \alpha = 1 \text{ in the main specification.}
\end{equation}
The variant with pseudo-counts in proportion to the stationary distribution replaces $\alpha$ in the numerator by $\alpha n \hat\pi_j$, where $\hat\pi$ is the vector of raw laggard frequencies.

\begin{proposition}[Perron--Frobenius; see \cite{seneta2006,norris1998}]
\label{prop:pf}
If $P$ is irreducible and aperiodic, it has a unique stationary distribution $\pi$ with $\pi P = \pi$, $\sum_i \pi_i = 1$ and $\pi_i > 0$ for all $i$, and $P^t \to \mathbf 1\pi$ as $t \to \infty$ from every initial distribution. The mean recurrence time of state $i$ is $\mu_i = 1/\pi_i$, the spectral gap is $\gamma = 1 - |\lambda_1|$, and the relaxation time is $1/\gamma$.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Test statistics.} The $\chi^2$ statistic compares the transition counts with the counts expected if $S_{t+1}$ is independent of $S_t$,
\begin{equation}
X^2 = \sum_{i,j} \frac{(n_{ij} - e_{ij})^2}{e_{ij}}, \qquad e_{ij} = \frac{n_{i\cdot}\, n_{\cdot j}}{N},
\end{equation}
with $(n-1)^2 = 100$ degrees of freedom. With $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{n-1}$ the non-Perron eigenvalues of $\tilde P$ ordered by modulus, the two spectral statistics are
\begin{equation}
T_{\max} = |\lambda_1|, \qquad T_{\mathrm{tr}} = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} |\lambda_k|^2.
\end{equation}
For a statistic $T$ with observed value $T_{\mathrm{obs}}$ and values $T_1, \dots, T_B$ on $B$ shuffled sequences, the permutation $p$-value is $p = B^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^B \mathbb I\{T_b \ge T_{\mathrm{obs}}\}$, with Monte Carlo standard error $\sqrt{p(1-p)/B}$.

\paragraph{Planted chains.} The power study simulates chains with transition matrix
\begin{equation}
P_a = (1-a)\,\mathbf 1\pi + aW, \qquad 0 < a < 1.
\end{equation}
For uniform persistence $W = I$, and for rotation $W$ is the cyclic permutation that sends each sector to the next; in both cases the non-Perron eigenvalues of $P_a$ have modulus $a$. For persistence in a single sector $j$, every row equals $\pi$ except row $j$, which is $(1-a)\pi + a e_j$; the largest non-Perron modulus is then below $a$, and Table~\ref{tab:power} reports it rather than $a$.

\section{Derivation of the Noise Radius}
\label{app:radius}

\paragraph{Non-Perron eigenvalues are the eigenvalues of the noise.} Write the estimate as $\hat P = \mathbf 1\pi + E$. Both $\hat P$ and $\mathbf 1\pi$ have rows that sum to one, so $E\mathbf 1 = 0$. Let $Q$ be any invertible matrix whose first column is $\mathbf 1$. Since $\hat P\mathbf 1 = \mathbf 1$ and $E\mathbf 1 = 0$, the first columns of $Q^{-1}\hat P Q$ and $Q^{-1}EQ$ are $e_1$ and $0$. The matrix $Q^{-1}\mathbf 1\pi Q = e_1(\pi Q)$ is non-zero only in its first row, so $Q^{-1}\hat PQ$ and $Q^{-1}EQ$ have the same lower-right $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ block. Both are block upper-triangular, so the non-Perron eigenvalues of $\hat P$ are exactly the eigenvalues of $E$ other than its structural zero. The question of how large they are is therefore a question about the spectrum of the noise matrix.

\paragraph{Variance profile.} Under the null, row $i$ of $\hat P$ is a multinomial proportion with $n_{i\cdot}$ trials and probabilities $\pi$, so $E_{ij}$ has mean zero and variance $\pi_j(1-\pi_j)/n_{i\cdot}$. With $n_{i\cdot} \approx N\pi_i$, the variance profile is
\begin{equation*}
V_{ij} = a_i b_j, \qquad a_i = \frac{1}{N\pi_i}, \qquad b_j = \pi_j(1-\pi_j).
\end{equation*}
A rank-one matrix $ab^\top$ has a single non-zero eigenvalue, $b^\top a$, so
\begin{equation*}
\rho(V) = \sum_j a_j b_j = \sum_j \frac{\pi_j(1-\pi_j)}{N\pi_j} = \frac1N \sum_j (1-\pi_j) = \frac{n-1}{N}.
\end{equation*}
For a random matrix with independent centred entries and variance profile $V$, the eigenvalue distribution is supported in the disk of radius $\sqrt{\rho(V)}$ \cite[Theorem~2.3]{cooketal2018}, which gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:radius}. Laplace smoothing multiplies row $i$ of the noise by $n_{i\cdot}/(n_{i\cdot} + \alpha n)$, which shrinks the radius slightly, by about 7\% for an average row in our sample.

\paragraph{Departures from the theorem.} The theorem's assumptions do not hold exactly here. Entries within a row are negatively correlated, because each row of $E$ sums to zero, and $n = 11$ is far from the asymptotic regime of the theorem. We therefore use the radius as a scale and base inference on simulation.

\paragraph{Monte Carlo check.} Under the permutation null (Section~\ref{sec:perm}), the largest non-Perron modulus of the smoothed estimate has mean 0.076 and 95th percentile 0.097 (0.098 for independently simulated sequences), against the radius $\sqrt{10/1{,}643} = 0.078$. For 1{,}000 matrices with independent uniform Dirichlet rows, the largest modulus has mean 0.182, 95th percentile 0.307 and 99th percentile 0.365, against the asymptotic Dirichlet radius $1/\sqrt{11} = 0.302$. In both cases the radius describes the typical size of the largest noise eigenvalue, with finite-sample spread around it.

\section{Why the Marchenko--Pastur Law Does Not Apply}
\label{app:mp}

The Marchenko--Pastur law describes the eigenvalues of a sample covariance matrix $C = L^{-1}XX^\top$, where $X$ is an $n \times L$ matrix of independent noise. When $n/L \to q$, the eigenvalues of $C$ fill the interval $[(1-\sqrt q)^2, (1+\sqrt q)^2]$ \cite{marchenkopastur1967}. A transition matrix differs from $C$ in every property the law relies on.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item \emph{Symmetry.} $C$ is symmetric, so its eigenvalues are real. $\tilde P$ is not symmetric, and three of its eigenvalue pairs are complex (Table~\ref{tab:eig}).
\item \emph{Sign of the eigenvalues.} $C$ is positive semi-definite, so its eigenvalues are non-negative. $\tilde P$ has eigenvalues with negative real parts.
\item \emph{A fixed eigenvalue.} Every row of $\tilde P$ sums to one, so $\mathbf 1$ is always an eigenvector with eigenvalue 1. The Perron eigenvalue is fixed by construction, not estimated.
\item \emph{How the noise enters.} In $C$ the noise enters through the product $XX^\top$. In $\hat P$ it is added to a fixed matrix, $\hat P = \mathbf 1\pi + E$ (Appendix~\ref{app:radius}), and its entries are bounded proportions with unequal variances.
\end{enumerate}
Comparing the eigenvalues of $\tilde P$ with a Marchenko--Pastur interval would therefore compare complex numbers with a range of real ones. The right comparison is with the spectrum of $E$, which is what Eq.~\eqref{eq:radius} describes.

\section{Robustness Details}
\label{app:robust}

\paragraph{Order-2 test.} The order-2 model conditions on the pair $(S_{t-1}, S_t)$. Of the 121 possible pairs, 120 occur, giving $120 \times 10 = 1{,}200$ free parameters against 110 for order 1. The parametric bootstrap simulates 2{,}000 order-1 chains of 1{,}644 days from $\hat P$ and from $\tilde P$ and recomputes the likelihood ratio on each. The null means are 1{,}060 (s.d.\ 44) and 1{,}110 (s.d.\ 42), against the observed 1{,}176.2.

\paragraph{Permutation schemes.} The main permutation shuffles the whole sequence. The block permutation shuffles within consecutive 21-day blocks, which preserves slow changes in the laggard frequencies, such as Energy's share rising to 0.35 in 2020. The stability tests shift the regime indicator circularly by a random number of days, which preserves the length and clustering of each regime.

\paragraph{Volatility-standardised state.} Each return is divided by $\hat\sigma_{i,t-1}$, the square root of an exponentially weighted average of past squared returns with decay 0.94, so that only information available on day $t-1$ is used. The first 60 days are dropped as burn-in, leaving 1{,}584 days.

\paragraph{Calendar definitions.} The consecutive-days check keeps a transition only if the two trading days are one calendar day apart, which removes Friday-to-Monday and post-holiday transitions. The option-expiry check drops transitions that start or end on a Friday between the 15th and 21st of the month, the third Friday. Weekly returns are compounded from Friday to Friday.

\paragraph{Macro groupings.} In the main grouping, Defensive, Growth and Cyclical are the laggard on 36.9\%, 25.7\% and 37.5\% of days; in the alternative grouping, on 26.7\%, 18.4\% and 54.9\%.

\section{Forecast Evaluation Details}
\label{app:oos}

\paragraph{Models.} Let $f_j(t)$ be the number of days up to $t$ on which sector $j$ was the laggard, and $C^{(k)}(t)$ the lag-$k$ count matrix up to $t$. The four forecasts of $S_{t+1}$ are
\begin{align*}
\text{Frequency:}\quad & p_j = \frac{f_j(t) + 1}{t + 1 + n}, \\
\text{Markov-1:}\quad & p_j = \frac{C^{(1)}_{S_t j}(t) + 1}{C^{(1)}_{S_t \cdot}(t) + n}, \\
\text{Markov-1, shrunk:}\quad & p_j = \frac{C^{(1)}_{S_t j}(t) + K\bar p_j}{C^{(1)}_{S_t \cdot}(t) + K}, \qquad K = 11, \\
\text{Lag-4:}\quad & p_j = \frac{C^{(4)}_{S_{t-3} j}(t) + 1}{C^{(4)}_{S_{t-3} \cdot}(t) + n},
\end{align*}
where $\bar p$ is the frequency forecast. In the rolling design, the counts use only the last 504 days.

\paragraph{Test.} For each model, $d_t$ is its log score minus that of the frequency forecast. The Diebold--Mariano statistic is $\bar d/\widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\bar d)$, with the standard error from a Newey--West estimator with Bartlett weights and five lags.
